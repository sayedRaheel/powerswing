Large AI training jobs move GPU power by tens of percent within a second, and synchronized jobs add these swings together into fast load ramps of the kind that ramp-rate limits on large loads are meant to prevent. Fill-type smoothing (filler GPU work or storage that adds power while the load falls) can remove these violations, but it has to be engaged before the fall arrives, and keeping it engaged costs energy. A forecaster can decide when to engage it. We show that what the forecaster is trained to predict matters. In a matched-pipeline ablation, a gradient-boosted classifier trained on a generic target, "a large power drop is coming", ranks the events a ramp limiter must catch poorly (average precision, AP, 0.58 on held-out NLR H100 data). Trained on the controller's own event, "a ramp-limit violation will occur while a limiter triggered now would be engaged", the same pipeline reaches AP 0.96 (difference 95% CI 0.28 to 0.49), against 0.67 for a no-learning rhythm-replay baseline of the same target. In a limiter simulation with a threshold chosen on validation data for a 90% removal target, the learned trigger removes 86.8% of test violations with the actuator engaged 45.6% of the time, while rhythm replay meets the target only by staying engaged 99.2% of the time, i.e. effectively always on. Across three public datasets (H100, B200 and V100 nodes; scripted benchmarks and labelled cluster jobs), the learned trigger ranks violations significantly better than rhythm replay on two datasets when the site is represented in training (95% cluster-bootstrap intervals exclude zero); the third is inconclusive. On a site never seen in training, the learned trigger is never better than rhythm replay in AP, and an hour of local data does not reliably recover the advantage. We report this as an open problem.
